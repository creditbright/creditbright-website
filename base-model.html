The Deal

Credit is borrowed money. Someone gives you money now. You agree to pay it back later. And for that privilege, you pay a cost.

That's the deal. Every credit card, every car loan, every mortgage, every line of credit. No matter how it's packaged or marketed, every credit product in Canada comes down to that same basic exchange.

You receive money. You pay a price for using it.

That price is usually called interest. And the rules of the deal are written down in a document called a credit agreement.

We'll get into both of those in detail. But first, credit reaches further into your life than you might expect, and that changes why this course matters.

Two Types of Credit

Every credit product in Canada falls into one of two categories. Once you see the difference, you'll recognise it everywhere.

INSTALLMENT

You borrow a fixed amount, once. You pay it back in regular, predictable payments over a set period of time. When it's done, it's done. The loan closes.

Think car loans. Mortgages. Student loans. Personal loans.

REVOLVING

You get a credit limit. You can borrow up to that limit, pay some or all of it back, and then borrow again. The credit revolves. It stays open as long as the account is active.

Think credit cards. Lines of credit.

This distinction matters because almost everything that follows in this course works differently depending on which type you're dealing with. How interest is charged. How payments are calculated. How agreements are structured. Two different systems, both worth understanding.

and the Six Key Terms

Every time you borrow money in Canada, you sign a credit agreement. It's the contract between you and the lender. It spells out everything: how much you're borrowing, what it costs, how you'll pay it back, and what happens if something goes wrong.

Loan Amount

The loan amount is the total money you're borrowing. This applies to installment loans: car loans, personal loans, mortgages. With revolving credit like credit cards, the equivalent is your credit limit, the maximum you can borrow at any time. Either way, the principle is the same: the more you borrow, the more interest you pay. Interest isn't a flat fee. It's calculated based on how much you owe. Every extra dollar you borrow adds to your cost.

Loan Term

The loan term is how long you have to pay back the loan. This applies to installment loans, where you agree upfront to a set repayment period. It could be months or it could be decades. Revolving credit like credit cards doesn't have a fixed term. You can carry a balance indefinitely, which is part of what makes it dangerous.

Interest isn't a one-time charge. It's calculated on your remaining balance on a regular basis, for as long as you owe money. The longer you're in debt, the more interest you pay.

That creates a trade-off.

Imagine a $10,000 loan at 6% interest. One option gives you 3 years to repay. The other gives you 6 years. The 6-year option has a lower monthly payment, but what happens to the total interest?

Interest Rate

The interest rate is the yearly cost of borrowing, expressed as a percentage. Think of it as the price tag on your loan.

If you borrow $10,000 at 5% interest, you're paying roughly $500 per year for the privilege of using that money. The bigger the loan, the more that percentage costs you.

Two mortgages. Same amount. Same term. Mortgage A charges 4% interest. Mortgage B charges 6% interest. How much more do you think that 2% difference costs on a $300,000 mortgage over 25 years?

When the numbers are this big, every fraction of a percent matters. This is why shopping for the best rate isn't optional.

Fixed vs Variable

Not all interest rates stay the same.

FIXED RATE

Locked in for the life of your loan. Your rate won't change, and neither will your payment. Predictable.

VARIABLE RATE

Can move up or down over time, which means your payment could increase. It usually starts lower than a fixed rate, but that lower starting rate comes with risk.

You're signing a mortgage. The lender offers you two options. A fixed rate of 5.5% that stays the same for five years. Or a variable rate starting at 4.5% that could go up or down. Which is the safer choice if your monthly budget is tight?

When you sign a credit agreement, know which type you're getting. If it's variable, make sure you could handle the payment if the rate rises.

Where Your Money Actually Goes

You've seen what the interest rate costs over the life of a loan. But there's something most people don't realise about how payments work on installment loans.

When you make a monthly payment, it feels like all of that money is going toward paying off your debt.

It's not.

Every payment gets split in two. Part goes to interest - the cost of borrowing. Part goes to your actual balance - the amount you still owe. That amount is called the principal.

And early in the loan, the split is not in your favour.

Take a $10,000 loan at 6% over 3 years. Your monthly payment is $304. Watch how the split changes over time.

Month 1 $304 / month
$50 interest $254 principal

As your balance shrinks, the interest portion drops and more of your money goes to the principal. By month 36, almost your entire payment is reducing what you owe.

This is why paying extra early in a loan has such a big impact. Every extra dollar you put toward the principal in those early months reduces the balance that interest is calculated on. That means less interest next month, and the month after that, and every month that follows.

The shift is slow at first. Then it accelerates.

With revolving credit like credit cards, it works differently. There is no fixed payment schedule. Interest is simply charged on whatever balance you carry. But the principle still holds: the larger your balance, the more interest you pay. Paying more than the minimum reduces that balance faster, which reduces the interest charged. In Section 3, you'll see exactly how powerful that one change can be.

Annual Percentage Rate (APR)

The APR is the real cost of borrowing. It takes the interest rate and adds in any fees the lender charges you to get the loan. That includes origination fees, application fees, broker fees, administrative charges, and in some cases, insurance premiums. It rolls everything into one number: the true yearly cost.

Why does this matter? Because two loans can have the same interest rate but very different total costs, depending on the fees.

The interest rate is the sticker price. The APR is what you actually pay.

You're comparing two car loans. Both are $10,000 over 3 years.

For installment loans like car loans, personal loans, and mortgages, the APR must be disclosed in your cost of borrowing statement. Look for it before you sign.

For revolving credit like credit cards, the annual interest rate and fees are listed separately in the information box on your agreement. There is no single APR number that rolls them together for you. That is where your Credit Calculator comes in. Either way, the principle is the same: never compare offers by interest rate alone. Always look at the full cost of borrowing.

Collateral

Collateral is something valuable that you pledge as security for a loan. A home. A car. A savings account. If you don't repay, the lender can take it.

This creates two categories of credit.

SECURED

Backed by an asset. Mortgages, car loans, home equity lines of credit (a loan secured by your home that works like revolving credit). Because the lender has something to fall back on, they typically offer lower interest rates.

UNSECURED

No asset pledged. Credit cards, personal loans, student loans. These usually cost more in interest, because the lender is taking on more risk.

Repayment Terms

Repayment terms are the rules that govern when you pay, how much you pay, and what happens if you don't.

Three things to understand here.

When you pay.

With installment loans, you have a fixed due date each month and the amount is the same every time. With revolving credit like credit cards, you receive a monthly statement showing what you owe. You then have a window of time, usually 21 to 25 days, called the grace period. Pay your full balance within that window and you pay no interest on those purchases. Miss it, and interest kicks in.

Your credit card statement closes on March 1st. Your grace period is 21 days. What's the last day you can pay your full balance to avoid interest?

The minimum payment.

With installment loans, your payment is fixed. With revolving credit, each statement shows a minimum payment. It's the smallest amount you can pay without penalty. It's tempting to pay just that. The Minimum Payment Trap

Credit cards are unsecured, which means higher interest rates. In Canada, most major credit cards charge around 19.99%.

Your monthly statement shows a minimum payment. It's usually 2% to 3% of your balance, or a fixed amount like $10, whichever is greater. It looks manageable. That's by design. Lenders set the minimum low because the longer you take to pay, the more interest they earn.

That's the minimum payment trap. It keeps you in debt for decades, quietly draining thousands of dollars in interest, while the number on your monthly statement looks perfectly manageable.

Knowing this changes how you look at that minimum payment number forever.

The minimum payment trap is a revolving credit problem. The solution is simple: pay more than the minimum. Even a small amount above the minimum changes everything.

But what if you're carrying more than one debt? A credit card here, a car loan there, maybe a personal loan or a line of credit. Each one has its own balance, its own interest rate, and its own minimum payment. When money is tight, it's hard to know where to focus.

There are two proven strategies. Both work for any combination of debts, revolving or installment. They work differently, and the right one depends on what motivates you.

The Avalanche Method

List all your debts. Make the minimum payment on every one of them. Then take every extra dollar you can find and throw it at the debt with the highest interest rate.

Why? Because that's the one costing you the most money every single month. Every dollar you put toward it reduces the balance that interest is calculated on. You saw this in Section 2: interest is calculated on your remaining balance. The higher the rate, the faster it grows. Killing the most expensive debt first stops the biggest drain.

When that debt is gone, take the money you were putting toward it and add it to the minimum payment on the next highest rate. Then the next. Each time you clear a debt, the amount you can throw at the next one gets bigger.

The avalanche saves you the most money over time. It is the mathematically optimal approach. But it requires patience, because the highest-rate debt isn't always the smallest balance. It might take months before you see an account hit zero.

CREDIT BRIGHT
Section 3 of 7
3 of 6

The Snowball Method

List all your debts. Make the minimum payment on every one of them. Then take every extra dollar you can find and throw it at the debt with the smallest balance.

Why? Because clearing one account completely, even a small one, gives you a real win. One fewer bill. One account at zero. One less thing to think about. That momentum matters.

When that debt is gone, take the money you were putting toward it and add it to the minimum payment on the next smallest balance. Then the next. The payments snowball, getting larger with every debt you clear.

You will pay more in total interest than with the avalanche, because you're not targeting the most expensive debt first. But for many people, the psychological boost of clearing an account keeps them going when the avalanche method might have them stalling.

Character: Your Credit Report

Character is the most important C. It answers one question: will you pay it back?

This isn't about who you are as a person. It's about your track record as a borrower. Do you pay on time? Have you ever failed to repay a debt? How long have you been borrowing?

Lenders answer those questions by looking at one place: your credit report.

Your Credit Report

Every time you borrow money, make a payment, miss a payment, or open a new account, that information is recorded. Not by the lender. By two national organizations called credit bureaus.

In Canada, the two credit bureaus are Equifax and TransUnion. They collect information from lenders, banks, phone companies, and other creditors across the country. They compile that information into a file about you.

That file is your credit report. It is the record of your Character. You may not have known it existed, but lenders have been reading it every time you apply for credit.

Payment history and credit utilization together account for nearly two thirds of your score. The single most powerful thing you can do is pay on time, every time. The second most powerful is keep your balances low relative to your limits.

What Damages Character

Late payments are the single biggest hit to your credit score. Even one payment more than 30 days late can stay on your report for up to six years in Canada.

Accounts in collections are one of the most severe marks. If a debt goes unpaid long enough, the lender hands it to a collection agency. That stays on your report for years.

A short credit history isn't damage in the same way, but it works against you. Lenders want a track record.

Hard vs Soft Inquiries

One more thing worth knowing. You can check your own credit report and score for free.

Both Equifax and TransUnion are required to give you a free copy of your credit report if you request one. You can also check your score for free through most major Canadian banks and through apps like Credit Karma.

Here's the important part: checking your own score does not hurt it. When you check your own score, it's called a soft inquiry. It doesn't appear on your report and lenders can't see it. When a lender checks your score because you've applied for credit, that's a hard inquiry, and it does appear on your report. Too many hard inquiries in a short period can lower your score slightly, because it looks like you're desperate for credit.

So check your score. Check it regularly. It's your file. You should know what's in it.

Capacity: Can You Afford It?

Capacity answers a different question: can you afford it?

This is about your income relative to your existing obligations. Lenders want to know that you earn enough to cover a new payment on top of everything you already owe. The less of your income that's already committed, the more room you have to borrow.

Income is not on your credit report. Lenders get it from your application. But there is one part of Capacity that does show up on your report: credit utilization.

Credit Utilization

Credit utilization is how much of your available revolving credit you're using. If your credit card limit is $10,000 and your balance is $8,000, that's 80% utilization. Lenders see that as a sign of financial stress.

Below 30% is the target. Divide your total credit card balances by your total limits.

Capital is your savings, investments, or other assets. It tells the lender that even if something goes wrong - a job loss, an emergency - you have resources to fall back on.

Capital is assessed from your application, not from the credit bureaus. Your savings don't appear on your credit report. But they matter to lenders, especially for large loans like mortgages.

Conditions

Conditions is the one C you can't control. It refers to the broader context of the loan: what you're borrowing for, the state of the economy, interest rate trends, and even the industry you work in.

A loan for a home purchase during a stable economy looks different to a lender than a loan for a speculative investment during a downturn. If a lender says no and nothing about your finances has changed, Conditions may be the reason.

Collateral

You already know this one from Section 2. Collateral is an asset you pledge as security for a loan. A home for a mortgage. A car for a car loan. If you stop paying, the lender can take it.

Secured loans are less risky for the lender, which is why they come with lower interest rates. Unsecured loans cost more because the lender has nothing to fall back on.

THE TRAPS

Buy Now, Pay Later

You've probably seen this at checkout. A company offers to split your purchase into smaller payments. No interest. No hassle.

And sometimes it is. If you make every payment on time and never overextend, BNPL can work fine.

The marketing tells one story. The fine print tells another.

Miss a payment and the fees hit fast. Some BNPL providers charge late fees, and some retroactively apply interest to the entire purchase, not just the missed payment. And because BNPL makes spending feel smaller, it's easy to stack multiple plans at once. Four purchases split into four payments each means sixteen payment deadlines. That's sixteen chances to miss one.

BNPL works like revolving credit without calling itself that. The payment revolves. The risk compounds. But it arrives without the same level of disclosure that credit cards are required to give you.

BNPL providers are increasingly reporting to credit bureaus. That means missed payments can show up on your credit report, just like a missed credit card payment.

You're buying a $400 jacket. The store offers BNPL: four payments of $100, no interest. You already have two other BNPL plans active. What's the real risk?

Payday Loans

Payday loans are short-term, small-dollar loans designed to tide you over until your next paycheque. They're easy to get. You walk in, show proof of income, and walk out with cash. No credit check. No waiting.

They are also one of the most expensive forms of borrowing that exists.

A typical payday loan in Canada charges around $14 per $100 borrowed, for a two-week term.

A payday lender charges $14 per $100 borrowed for two weeks. If you translated that into an annual interest rate, what do you think it would be?

The real danger isn't just the cost. It's the cycle. If you can't repay the loan plus fees by your next paycheque, you take out another one to cover it. That's the payday loan trap: borrowing to repay borrowing, with fees stacking every cycle.

In Canada, payday lending is regulated at the provincial level, and the rules vary across the country. Some provinces cap the fees lenders can charge. Others have stricter limits on rollovers and repeat borrowing. The federal Criminal Code also sets a maximum cost of borrowing for payday loans.

Predatory Offers

"Pre-approved" offers. Being pre-approved doesn't mean the terms are good. It means you've been targeted. Always check the interest rate, the fees, and the fine print before accepting anything. If it's an installment loan, look for the APR. If it's revolving credit, enter the numbers into your Credit Calculator and see the real cost.

Introductory 0% rates. Some credit cards offer 0% interest for the first six or twelve months. That can be genuinely useful. But if you carry a balance past the introductory period, the rate jumps, often to 20% or higher, and in some cases, interest is charged retroactively on the full original balance.

"No credit check" offers. If a lender doesn't check your credit, it usually means they've built the risk into the price. Higher rates. Higher fees.

You already know the tool that cuts through all of this. For installment loans, it's the APR. Find it on the disclosure statement before you sign. For revolving credit, look at the interest rate and the fee schedule together, or enter the numbers into your Credit Calculator and let it show you the true annual cost. Either way, do the math before you commit. The numbers tell you more than any marketing language ever will.

Co-signing

A co-signer is someone who agrees to be legally responsible for someone else's debt if that person can't pay.

It's usually a favour. A parent co-signing a child's first car loan. A friend helping someone who can't qualify on their own. The intention is generous.

But here's what co-signing actually means.

The debt appears on your credit report. If the borrower misses a payment, it goes on your record too. If they stop paying entirely, the lender comes to you for the full amount. Your credit score takes the hit. And in some cases, the lender can come after you without even trying to collect from the borrower first.

Three paths. Three very different outcomes. Sometimes co-signing is the right decision, a parent helping a child get started, a partner supporting a shared goal. But before you co-sign anything, understand exactly what you're agreeing to. The debt appears on your credit report. If the borrower misses a payment, your score takes the hit. You're not just vouching for someone. You're taking on their obligation as your own.

Your Rights

You are not powerless in this system.

You have the right to see your credit report. Free, from both bureaus.

You have the right to dispute errors. If something on your credit report is wrong - a payment marked as late that you paid on time, an account that isn't yours, a balance that's incorrect - you can file a dispute with the credit bureau. They are legally required to investigate within 30 days. If they can't verify the information, they must correct or remove it.

This matters more than most people realise. Errors on credit reports are not rare. And an error you don't catch can cost you a higher interest rate, a declined application, or worse.

You notice a payment marked as late that you know you paid on time. What are you entitled to do?

You have the right to know why you were declined. If a lender turns you down for credit, they must tell you why. That information helps you understand what to work on.

You have the right to be free from harassment. Collection agencies in Canada must follow strict rules. They cannot call you at unreasonable hours, threaten you, or use abusive language. If a collection agency crosses the line, you can file a complaint with your provincial consumer protection office.

You have the right to file a complaint. Start with the institution, then escalate to the relevant ombudsman, then to FCAC.

Strengthen Your Character

Character is your track record as a borrower. It's the most important C, and it's built one month at a time.

In Section 4, you learned that payment history is the single biggest factor in your credit score, carrying roughly 35% of the weight. You learned that late payments can stay on your report for up to six years. And you learned that a longer credit history gives lenders more evidence to trust you.

Here's how to build that evidence.

Pay every bill on time. This is the single most powerful thing you can do for your credit. One on-time payment doesn't transform your file overnight. But twelve months of them starts to build a pattern that lenders trust. If you can, set up automatic payments or calendar reminders. Remove the chance of a late payment entirely.

Get a secured credit card. If you're building credit for the first time, or rebuilding after a difficult period, a secured card is designed for exactly this situation. You put down a deposit, and that becomes your credit limit. Use it for small purchases and pay it off every month. Every on-time payment is reported to the credit bureaus. It's a tool for building Character one month at a time. Over time, many issuers will upgrade you to a regular unsecured card.

Report your rent payments. You're probably already making the largest regular payment in your life and getting no credit for it. Services like FrontLobby and Borrowell RentAdvantage can report your rent payments to the credit bureaus on your behalf. Your landlord may need to opt in, or you can sign up directly depending on the service. If you're already paying on time, this turns a payment you're already making into a credit-building tool.

Become an authorized user. If someone you trust, a parent, a partner, a family member, has a credit card with a long history of on-time payments, they can add you as an authorized user. Their account history on that card may appear on your credit report, giving you the benefit of their track record. You don't even need to use the card. This works especially well for newcomers to Canada or young adults building credit for the first time. Just make sure the primary cardholder manages the account responsibly, because their mistakes would show up on your report too.

Strengthen Your Capacity

Utilization has no memory. Unlike late payments, which stay on your report for up to six years, utilization resets every time your lender reports your balance to the credit bureaus. That's typically once per billing cycle. Pay your balance down today, and the improvement can show up within weeks. A single payment can change your number next cycle.

This makes utilization one of the fastest ways to improve how lenders see you.

Pay before the statement date. Your lender reports your balance on a specific day, usually when your statement closes. If you pay down your balance before that date, the lower number is what gets reported. Even if you use the card regularly, timing your payment can keep your reported utilization low.

Request a credit limit increase. If your balance stays the same but your limit goes up, your utilization ratio drops instantly. Same debt, better ratio. Some issuers let you request an increase online. Be aware that some may do a hard inquiry, but the utilization benefit typically outweighs the small inquiry impact.

Spread spending across cards. If you have more than one credit card, spreading your purchases across them keeps any single card from looking maxed out. Utilization is calculated both per-card and across all cards, so balance matters everywhere.

Don't close old cards. Closing a credit card reduces your total available credit. If your balances stay the same, your utilization ratio goes up. Keep old accounts open and active, even if you only use them for a small purchase once a month.

Strengthen Your Collateral Position

For most Canadians, the biggest collateral question is a mortgage. Lenders use a specific formula: the Total Debt Service ratio (TDS). It measures all your debt payments as a percentage of your gross income.

Canadian lenders typically require TDS under 44%. CMHC, the Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation, will not insure a mortgage above this threshold.

Knowing your number before you apply puts you in control. If you're above the threshold, you can see exactly what needs to change.
