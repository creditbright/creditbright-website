The Trap You Already Know

In the Foundation Course, you saw what happens when you pay only the minimum on a credit card. A $5,000 balance at 19.99% takes over 30 years to pay off. You end up paying more than $7,700 in interest alone. More than double what you originally borrowed.

That is the minimum payment trap. It works because the numbers on your monthly statement look manageable. $100 a month feels fine. But behind that small number, interest is compounding on your remaining balance every single month.

Avalanche and Snowball: A Quick Refresh

If you are carrying more than one debt, the Foundation Course taught you two strategies for deciding where to focus your extra money.

The Avalanche. Pay minimums on everything. Put every extra dollar toward the debt with the highest interest rate. This saves you the most money over time. It is the mathematically optimal approach.

The Snowball. Pay minimums on everything. Put every extra dollar toward the debt with the smallest balance. This gives you the fastest win. One fewer bill. One account at zero. The momentum keeps you going.

Both strategies require the same thing: extra money focused on one debt at a time. Neither works if you only pay the minimum on everything.

You have three debts: a $1,200 store card at 12%, a $4,000 credit card at 19.99%, and a $10,000 car loan at 6%. You have $200 extra per month. Under the avalanche, where does that $200 go?

The Debt Audit

Before you can choose a strategy, you need to see the full picture. This is your debt audit. Every debt. Every rate. Every payment.

Write down each debt you carry right now. For each one, note the balance, the interest rate, and your current monthly payment. If it is a credit card or line of credit, note the minimum payment. If it is an installment loan, note the fixed monthly payment.

Once you see all your debts in one place, two numbers matter most. The first is your total monthly cost — the sum of all your minimum payments. That is the floor. You must pay at least that much every month or your credit takes a hit.

The second number is your extra budget. This is the money above and beyond your total minimum payments that you can commit to attacking one debt at a time. Even $50. Even $25. The Calculator shows you what that extra money does over time.

You complete your debt audit. Your total monthly minimums add up to $425. Your monthly income after essentials is $600. What is your extra budget for debt repayment?

The Acceleration

A lower interest rate. Fewer accounts. A lump sum that wipes out a balance. These are the tools that can cut months or years off your timeline.

Balance Transfers

A balance transfer moves an existing credit card balance from one card to another, usually to take advantage of a lower promotional interest rate. In Canada, many cards offer 0% or low-rate promotional periods of 6 to 12 months on transferred balances.

The idea is simple. If you are paying 19.99% on a $5,000 balance, and you can move that balance to a card charging 0% for 10 months, every dollar you pay during that window goes straight to principal. No interest eating into your payments.

The catch. Balance transfers are not free. Most Canadian cards charge a transfer fee of 1% to 3% of the balance. On $5,000, that is $50 to $150, added to your new balance on day one. And when the promotional period ends, the rate jumps — often to 19.99% or higher. If you have not paid off the balance by then, you are back where you started, minus the transfer fee.

You transfer a $4,000 balance to a card offering 0% for 10 months with a 2% transfer fee. When does a balance transfer save you money?

Debt Consolidation

Debt consolidation replaces several debts with one. You take out a single loan — usually a personal loan or a line of credit — and use it to pay off all your existing balances. Instead of three or four payments at different rates, you have one payment at one rate.

In Canada, personal consolidation loans from banks and credit unions typically range from 7% to 15%, depending on your credit profile. If you are carrying credit card debt at 19.99%, even a consolidation loan at 12% saves you money on every dollar.

Why it works. A lower average interest rate means more of your payment goes to principal. One payment means less chance of missing a due date. And a fixed term means you have a guaranteed payoff date — something revolving credit never gives you.

Why it fails. Consolidation only works if you close the old accounts or stop using them. If you consolidate $15,000 of credit card debt into a loan but then run the cards back up, you end up with the loan plus the new card balances. That is worse than where you started.

You consolidate three credit cards totalling $12,000 at 19.99% into a personal loan at 10% over 4 years. What is the biggest risk?

Refinancing

Refinancing means replacing an existing loan with a new one on better terms. This applies most often to mortgages, but it can work for car loans and personal loans too.

In Canada, mortgage refinancing is common when rates have dropped since you signed your original term. If you locked in at 5.5% and current rates are 4.2%, refinancing could save you thousands over the remaining amortization.

The Canadian catch: penalties. Breaking a fixed-rate mortgage in Canada before the term is up triggers a penalty. It is usually the greater of three months’ interest or the interest rate differential (IRD) — a calculation based on the difference between your rate and the current rate for the remaining term. On a large mortgage, this penalty can be $10,000 or more.

That does not mean refinancing is a bad idea. It means you must do the math. If the interest savings over the new term exceed the penalty, refinancing wins. If they do not, you wait until renewal.

You are two years into a five-year fixed mortgage at 5.5%. Current rates are 4.2%. Your bank quotes a $6,000 penalty to break the term. What should you do?

Windfalls

A windfall is any lump sum of money that arrives outside your regular income. A tax refund. An annual bonus. An inheritance. A gift. Money from selling something you no longer need.

In Canada, the average tax refund is around $2,000. For someone carrying high-interest debt, that $2,000 can save hundreds of dollars in future interest if it goes to the right place.

Where to put it. The same logic as your monthly extra budget, but amplified. If you are using the avalanche, the windfall goes to your highest-rate debt. If you are using the snowball, it goes to your smallest balance. If the windfall is large enough to clear a balance entirely, even better — one fewer debt, one fewer payment, and that freed-up money rolls into the next target.

The temptation. A $2,000 tax refund feels like found money. The pull to spend it is real. But here is the math: $2,000 applied to a credit card at 19.99% saves you roughly $400 in interest per year. That is a 20% return on your money, guaranteed, risk-free. No investment in the world reliably beats that.

You receive a $2,000 tax refund. You are carrying a $3,000 credit card at 19.99% and a $1,500 store card at 12%. You are using the avalanche. Where does the $2,000 go?

Four Tools, One Goal

You now have four acceleration strategies, each working differently.

Balance Transfer
Rate
Temporarily eliminates or reduces interest so every payment hits principal. Best for credit card debt you can pay off within the promotional window.
Consolidation
Simplify
Replaces multiple debts with one at a lower rate. Best when you are juggling several high-rate balances and want one fixed payment with a guaranteed end date.
Refinancing
Terms
Replaces an existing loan with better terms. Best for mortgages when rates have dropped significantly. Must account for the penalty of breaking a fixed term in Canada.
Windfall
Lump Sum
Applies a one-time payment to your target debt. Best used on high-rate debt for maximum interest savings. A $2,000 refund on a 19.99% card earns you a guaranteed 20% return.

These tools do not replace the avalanche or snowball. They work alongside them. You choose a strategy for which debt to target. You use these tools to change the terms of the debt itself.

One Credit Card, One Balance

Scenario
Priya has a $6,000 balance on a single credit card at 19.99%. She has no other debt. She can afford to pay $250 per month. She just received a balance transfer offer: 0% for 12 months, with a 2% transfer fee.

This is the simplest case. One debt, one potential move. The question is whether the balance transfer saves her money.

At $250 per month, Priya can pay $3,000 over 12 months. The transfer fee adds $120 to her balance, making it $6,120. After 12 months at 0%, she still owes $3,120. When the rate jumps to 19.99%, she is back to paying interest on that remaining amount.

Without the transfer, at 19.99%, roughly $100 of her $250 payment goes to interest every month in the early stages. So after 12 months, her balance would still be around $4,500.

The balance transfer puts her $1,380 further ahead after one year. The $120 fee paid for itself many times over.

Multiple Debts, Feeling Overwhelmed

Scenario
Marcus has four debts: a $2,000 store card at 12%, a $5,000 Visa at 19.99%, a $3,000 personal line of credit at 8%, and a $15,000 car loan at 6.5%. His total minimums are $580. He has $150 extra per month. He is tired of juggling four payments and wants a simpler plan.

Marcus has two good options. The first is straightforward: use the avalanche. Put the $150 toward his Visa (highest rate at 19.99%) while paying minimums on everything else. When the Visa is gone, roll that payment into the store card.

The second option is consolidation. If Marcus can get a personal loan at 9% for the three non-car debts ($10,000 total), he replaces three payments with one. His weighted average rate on those three debts drops from about 15% to 9%. And he gets a fixed end date.

The decision comes down to discipline. If Marcus can stick to the avalanche for 18 months without missing a payment, it works. If he finds juggling four payments stressful and is at risk of slipping, consolidation simplifies his life and still saves him money.

Marcus has four debts and $150 extra per month. Under what circumstance is consolidation likely the better choice over the avalanche?

A Mortgage and a Windfall

Scenario
Aisha has a $320,000 mortgage at 5.2% with 3 years left on a 5-year fixed term. She also has a $4,000 credit card balance at 19.99%. She just received a $3,000 inheritance.

Aisha might be tempted to put the $3,000 toward her mortgage. After all, it is her largest debt. But rate matters more than size.

$3,000 applied to a 19.99% credit card saves her roughly $600 per year in interest. That same $3,000 applied to a 5.2% mortgage saves about $156 per year. The credit card wins by a factor of nearly four.

What about refinancing the mortgage? Current rates would need to be significantly below 5.2% to justify the penalty of breaking her fixed term. With 3 years left, the IRD penalty could be substantial. Better to wait for renewal, negotiate a lower rate then, and use the inheritance where it does the most good: the credit card.

Aisha has a $320,000 mortgage at 5.2% and a $4,000 credit card at 19.99%. She receives a $3,000 inheritance. Where should it go?

The Decision Framework

Every debt situation is different. But the decision logic is the same every time.

Step 1: Audit. List every debt. Balance, rate, payment. Open the Credit Calculator and enter them all.

Step 2: Find your extra budget. Income after essentials minus total minimums. Even a small number matters.

Step 3: Choose your base strategy. Avalanche saves the most money. Snowball gives the fastest win. Both work. Pick the one you will stick with.

Step 4: Look for acceleration. Can you transfer a high-rate balance to a promotional rate and pay it off in time? Can you consolidate multiple debts into a single lower-rate loan? Is a windfall coming that you can apply to your target debt? Is your mortgage up for renewal and rates have dropped?

Step 5: Test it in the Calculator. Before you commit to any move, enter the numbers. The Calculator does not guess. It shows you the exact cost of every option.

You can now look at any combination of debts and choose the right strategy. You have the framework, the tools, and the Calculator to test any move before you make it.

What Goes Wrong

These are the traps that catch people who know the strategies but do not protect themselves against the risks.

Re-spending after consolidation
You consolidate $15,000 of credit card debt into a personal loan. The cards are now at zero. It feels like you have room to breathe. You start using them again. Six months later, you have the loan plus $4,000 in new card debt. You are deeper in than when you started. The fix: stop using the credit cards. Keep the accounts open for their credit reporting benefits, but avoid adding new balances until you have paid off the consolidated loan.
The promotional rate expiring
You transfer $5,000 to a 0% card for 12 months. You pay $200 a month, clearing $2,400 by the end. The remaining $2,600 now sits at 22.99%. You are paying more interest than before you transferred. The fix: calculate your required monthly payment before transferring. If you cannot clear the balance in time, the transfer may not be worth it.
Ignoring the penalty math on refinancing
You break a fixed-rate mortgage in Canada to save 1% on your rate. The IRD penalty is $12,000. Over the remaining term, the rate savings total $8,000. You paid $12,000 to save $8,000. The fix: always use the Calculator. Enter the penalty as an upfront fee on the new offer and compare total costs.
Spending the windfall
You receive a $2,000 tax refund. You tell yourself you will put it toward debt, but first you buy a new phone and take a weekend trip. The refund is gone. The debt remains. The fix: apply the windfall the day it arrives. Do not let it sit in your chequing account. Move it to the debt immediately.

When to Ask for Help

Everything in this course assumes you can manage your debt on your own with the right tools and strategies. For many Canadians, that is true. But not for everyone.

If your total debt payments exceed 40% of your gross income, you are in a zone where lenders see risk. You should too. This is where strategies alone may not be enough.

If you are missing payments regularly despite your best efforts, the problem may be structural. Your income may not support your debt load. That is not a failure. It is a situation that requires professional support.

If you are losing sleep over debt, that matters. Financial stress affects your health, your relationships, and your ability to make good decisions.

In Canada, non-profit credit counselling is available at no cost. Credit Counselling Canada is the national association. A credit counsellor can review your full situation, negotiate with your creditors on your behalf, and in some cases arrange a Debt Management Plan (DMP) that consolidates your payments at reduced or zero interest.

The Government of Canada provides a comprehensive guide to debt help and credit counselling resources: canada.ca/debt-help

If your debt is more severe, a Licensed Insolvency Trustee (LIT) can explain options like a Consumer Proposal, which legally reduces what you owe, or bankruptcy as a last resort.

Asking for help is not giving up. It is choosing a different tool for a harder problem.
